Case Study 09 — Mirror

Mirror E-Commerce Site & Design System

Mirror, a 400-location clothing retailer with no prior online presence, needed a logo, a design system, and a responsive e-commerce site built from the ground up — one that solved a very specific problem: shoppers who couldn't find their size in-store were walking to competitors instead.

I later revisited this project to explore how the same research findings — the filter-hunting, the sizing anxiety, the missed review links — would shape the product differently if I were designing it today, with AI-assisted discoverability built into the system from day one. That exploration is woven through the case study below.

The Challenge

Losing customers to stores that let them shop online

Mirror is an international clothing retailer with over 400 locations across 32 countries, built on the belief that clothing shouldn't be expensive or permanent — styles should change as often as the shopper wants. But when customers couldn't find their size in-store, they had nowhere else to go: Mirror had never had an e-commerce site. Shoppers were walking straight to competitors who offered one, and Mirror's warehouses were piling up with leftover inventory that was hard to move without an online channel. I was brought on to design Mirror's first digital presence — a new logo, a design system, and a responsive e-commerce site — from scratch.

Revisiting Mirror — AI Discoverability

If I designed Mirror today, I'd bake AI into the system, not bolt it on

Every one of Mirror's biggest usability findings — the filter-hunting, the sizing anxiety, the missed checkout button, the buried review link — points to a moment where the interface asked the user to do work the system could do for them. Here are the five AI-assisted patterns I'd layer onto the same research, each tied to a specific section below.

01

Natural-language search & filter

70% of testers hunted for filters immediately — a validated top need. Rather than expanding the existing multi-attribute filter UI further, a natural-language search bar ("affordable blue dresses for a beach wedding, true to size") lets shoppers describe what they want instead of toggling through size, body type, and price one at a time.

02

Suggested prompts for size & fit

Interviews surfaced a direct ask for models of different body types and clear sizing detail. On product pages, one-click prompts — "Show me on a similar body type," "What size should I order?" — turn the model-detail data already in the system into an interactive fit aid instead of static text.

03

Proactive nudges at checkout

Removing the redundant checkout button cut miss-clicks and sped up the flow by 30% — proof that less noise at this step wins. Any AI layered in here has to follow the same rule: quiet, contextual nudges ("this pairs with your recent browsing") rather than pop-ups that re-clutter an already-simplified path.

04

AI-assisted review synthesis

Testers couldn't find the review link until heatmap data showed me where to move it. A small affordance next to the (now-discoverable) review link — "Summarize what reviewers with my body type said" — solves the same findability problem while adding a reason to actually open it.

05

Autocomplete across the input system

Mirror's input fields — shipping address, size, search — were built into the design system from a blank page, with no legacy patterns to work around. That's the cleanest possible starting point for invisible, not intrusive, autocomplete: shipping addresses and past sizes pre-filled, a simple tab-to-accept or ignore-to-continue, no forced interaction.

Research & Discovery

7 interviews, 2 opportunities, 2 hard constraints

I conducted 7 remote interviews with avid to occasional online shoppers. Every one of them shopped online mainly because they were short on time, and preferred sites that displayed product information and shipping estimates with high accuracy.

Opportunity

Give current customers more ways to shop from Mirror.

Opportunity

Open the doors to a whole new pool of online shoppers.

Constraint

Mirror had never had a website or online presence before.

Constraint

There were no existing products online to gather customer feedback from.

Experience Design

3 principles that guided every decision

From the 7 interviews, I identified 3 motivating factors behind why people shop online at all, then checked each against Mirror's business goals to define the principles that shaped the design.

Familiarity

Users shopped more from sites tied to a physical store they already trusted.

Efficiency

Users shopped online because they were short on time and wanted speed.

Delight

Users also shopped online for the thrill of it — discovery, not just utility.

A storyboard and empathy map helped me understand my persona Emma's worries and hesitations at each step, and a feature prioritization diagram (value vs. cost vs. difficulty) helped me focus on the easy wins and foundational elements first.

Mapping Emma's happy path revealed the core flow; mapping an alternative path revealed the need for screens I hadn't planned for yet — reviews, more filters, and login options.

Information Architecture & Wireframes

Card sorting to sitemap to hi-fidelity

A card sort with 5 participants on the women's category (the largest, most complex) produced 11 clear subcategories. I sketched 3 low-fidelity home page layouts on paper, tested them with 2 users, and carried the winning layout into the rest of the site.

Mirror had no existing logo. I sketched several directions, tested them with 2 users for warmth and modernity, and landed on a lower-case wordmark with soft, rounded edges — a deliberate move away from the sharp wordmarks most competitors used, to feel inclusive and friendly rather than aspirational.

UI Design

A design system built from a blank page

With no prior digital presence, there were no existing UI patterns to reuse. I built Mirror's design system in Figma — headers, footers, buttons, and input fields for both desktop and mobile — so every screen that came after stayed cohesive.

Testing & Iteration

3 rounds of usability testing in Maze

I gave testers a specific task — order a pair of purple kids' pants, size 9 — designed to surface whether people reached for filters unprompted. They did.

70%

Went straight for filters

Confirmed filters were a top, non-negotiable need — but most testers couldn't locate the review link right away.

30%

Got stuck at the filter stage

A missing scroll affordance in the prototype blocked them entirely — an easy fix once I found it, but a reminder that even the smallest interaction detail can stop a flow cold.

Outcomes & Lessons

What I'd carry forward

What motivates users to shop online — familiarity, efficiency, delight — should guide every design decision, not just the ones about layout.

Building a design system first made it possible to design a cohesive e-commerce site quickly, with every key element visible in one place.

Placement matters as much as content — users expected the review link immediately, and its position directly affected purchase confidence.

If I had more time: a checkout progress indicator, product videos showing garments in movement, and a lightweight mobile app for frequent shoppers.
